# How to judge a vocal separation by ear

A useful listening test ends in a decision about a particular track: “I can rehearse over this instrumental” or “I need a different vocal take for the exposed intro.” You can make that decision with the original mix, both separated tracks (stems), and a player that lets you replay a few seconds. Here is a repeatable way to do it.

## 1. Mark three short loops in the original

Choose the passages **before** listening to the stems. Write down the start and end times so you can compare the same musical event on each pass.

| Loop | Pick a passage where… | Mark one sound you can follow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A — the voice enters | The band plays a repeating pattern and the singer comes in halfway through. | A guitar chord, hi-hat, or piano note that continues under the first word. |
| B — the line ends | A held syllable fades and there is a brief gap before the next line. | The final consonant, the vocal echo, and the first drum hit after the line. |
| C — the arrangement fills out | Lead voice, harmonies, and bright instruments overlap. | One backing-vocal phrase and one repeated snare or cymbal hit. |

Five to ten seconds per loop is usually enough. If the excerpt has no gap between lines, leave B untested. An instrumental intro alone gives you no evidence about how the separator handles a singing voice.

## 2. Test what disappeared from the backing track

Play loop A in the original, then in the instrumental. Keep your attention on the **instrument** you marked. Does its attack or sustain change exactly when the singer enters? Replay the original. If the guitar already gets quieter there, the change may belong to the arrangement. If it rings through the word in the original but drops out in the instrumental, listen to the vocal stem for the missing piece of guitar. Hearing it there strengthens the diagnosis.

Next, play B and C in the instrumental and try to write down any words you can understand. Record “the last two words of the chorus are intelligible” or “only a diffuse echo follows the line.” Those observations distinguish a surviving lead from a faint tail. If you want the backing harmonies for rehearsal, record separately whether they survived.

## 3. Test what came along with the voice

Play loop B in the vocal stem. Follow the singer all the way through the final *s* or *t*. Switch to the original at the same timestamp. If that consonant is clear in the mix but breaks up in the stem, mark the word and time. Then play the gap after it. Count any repeated clicks against the beat in the original. Clicks that line up with the hi-hat suggest percussion bleed; a single breath in the same place in both versions may be part of the performance.

In loop C, follow one held vowel. Listen for a steady note turning grainy or pulsing on drum hits. Compare with the original for vibrato, vocal effects, or an intentionally chopped delivery before attributing it to separation. Keep the playback level comfortable and roughly match the loudness of the sound you are following. A large jump in level makes small defects seem more important.

## 4. Make the decision in the actual arrangement

- **Rehearsal backing track:** Sing the marked line over the instrumental at practice volume. Can you choose your own entrance, or does the residual lead audibly double you? Does the missing instrument take away a timing cue?
- **Vocal for a remix:** Place the isolated line over the intended beat, then audition its most exposed entrance or ending. A snare carried over from the source may disappear under a new snare yet sound like an accidental extra hit in a breakdown.
- **Transcription:** Replay the hardest word or harmony without looking at the lyrics. If you still cannot distinguish the syllable or pitch, mark that passage as unresolved. A stem can be useful for the rest of the verse.

### Example listening note

*Illustrative timestamps, not a measurement from a published recording.*

| Passage | What I heard | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:32–0:38, verse entrance | The guitar sustains through “stay” in the original. Its attack survives in the instrumental, but the sustain dips under that word. | Fine for rehearsal: I can still hear the downbeat. Flag the dip if the backing will play alone. |
| 1:06–1:12, chorus ending | Two hi-hat ticks remain after the final vocal. They match the original rhythm and clash with the new beat during its stop. | Keep the sung phrase. Trim or fade the gap and replay the final consonant before accepting the edit. |

For your own notes, use one line per finding: **timestamp · stem · sound in original · sound in stem · consequence for this use**. If the only chorus you checked was quiet and the final chorus adds a choir, leave the final chorus unverified until you hear it.
